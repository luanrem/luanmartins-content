My homelab worked. Twelve services, up for months, doing exactly what I set them
up to do. I tore the whole thing down anyway.

## Why tear down something that works

Because "works" was doing a lot of hiding.

It had been assembled by hand over about two years. Every service was configured
in the moment, with decisions that made sense at the time and were never written
down anywhere. Which meant that changing anything started the same way: open the
config files and try to reconstruct what past-me had been thinking.

That is a system that works and cannot be changed. And a system I am afraid to
change is one I stop maintaining — I had already caught myself avoiding an
upgrade because I was not confident I could put it back.

## The rule

One rule, and it decided everything else: **if it is not in a repository, it
does not exist.**

Proxmox underneath. Terraform describing the machines. Ansible configuring them.
Traefik handling TLS and routing. Tailscale for access, so nothing is exposed to
the open internet and there is no port-forwarding rule to get subtly wrong.

## What it actually cost

More than a weekend, if I am honest. The infrastructure came up quickly. What
took the time was the long tail — the one service with a hand-tuned config
nobody remembers writing, the certificate that turned out to be renewed by a
cron job on a machine that was not supposed to matter.

Finding those was most of the value. They were exactly the things that would
have broken during a real recovery, at the worst possible moment.

## What I got back

Recreating a service is a command. Upgrades are testable, because I can stand up
a copy, break it, and throw it away. And the part I underestimated: I stopped
being careful. Not careless — just no longer treating the machines as fragile
things to be worked around.

## What I got wrong

Backups came last. There was a stretch of a few weeks where the infrastructure
was fully reproducible and the data inside it was not, which is genuinely the
worst configuration of the two — because everything about it feels safe. The
machines could come back; what they held could not.
